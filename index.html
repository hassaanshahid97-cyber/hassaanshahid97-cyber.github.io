01 / Research

How wrong is a fast surrogate allowed to be?

Sole-authored work on physics-informed surrogate models for nonlinear transient conjugate heat transfer — and on the two questions that decide whether such a model is usable rather than merely demonstrable.

Under editorial review DOI 10.5281/zenodo.21788995 ↗ Code & data ↗ ✓ 104/104 claims audited MIT · CC BY 4.0 Reproduces in 2 core-hours

Seed variability and calibrated error bounds for physics-informed conjugate heat transfer surrogates

Hassaan Shahid · sole author · submitted to Heat Transfer Engineering (Taylor & Francis), August 2026 · Preprint archived on Zenodo

Physics-informed neural networks are routinely proposed as fast surrogates for heat transfer problems too expensive to solve inside a design or control loop. Two questions about them are rarely answered. This paper answers both on a verified reference problem, and publishes every number, every script, and an audit that re-checks the manuscript against the stored results.

QUESTION 01

How much of the reported accuracy survives a change of random seed?

QUESTION 02

How large an error allowance does a downstream decision rule need to stay safe?

The solver is verified before a single surrogate is trained

If this step failed, nothing downstream would be interpretable — surrogate error could not be separated from discretisation error. Two independent checks, both passing.

Why machine precision, not truncation error? For a linear k(T), the arithmetic-mean face conductivity reproduces ∫k dT exactly, so the scheme carries no linearisation error in the diffusive flux. Backward Euler was chosen over Crank–Nicolson deliberately — it is L-stable and does not oscillate on the sharp boundary-data transient. For a verification reference, that robustness is worth more than formal order when the time step is cheap. Discretisation error sits two to three orders of magnitude below surrogate error, so it cannot contaminate any comparison that follows.

The supervised model drives its training loss to 1.1 × 10⁻⁵, fitting its four points essentially perfectly. Where it lands between those points still depends strongly on initialisation — the residual removes that freedom. For an engineering deployment this may matter more than the mean: a surrogate whose accuracy depends on the random seed is hard to qualify, because the artefact you verified is not obviously the artefact you will use.

An accurate surrogate is not automatically a safe one

The surrogate was used as a hard inequality constraint in a coolant scheduler, across four independently trained models. An optimiser does not evaluate the model at a random point — it evaluates it wherever the constraint looks most nearly satisfied, so any optimism there is spent immediately.

The obvious refinement does not work. Calibrating only near the constraint boundary — where the optimiser actually selects — sounds strictly better. It gives 13.2 %, statistically indistinguishable from no allowance at all. Two effects combine: the band holds only 26–27 of 120 calibration points, so at α = 0.05 the conformal rank is ⌈28 × 0.95⌉ = 27 and δ becomes the maximum of a small sample; and a band in predicted margin is not the selection distribution the optimiser induces. Conditioning on the wrong variable discards most of the sample without correcting the bias it was meant to correct. This negative result is recorded because it is the natural first thing to try.

Stated carefully: 28× is modest, and modest for a good reason — the reference problem is a tridiagonal solve, about the cheapest transient PDE solve there is. What the demonstration establishes is the structural property the large speedups rest on: the same 7 345-parameter network would cost the same 2.24 ms whether trained against this solver or against a three-dimensional computation costing hours. No loss weight was tuned at any point in this study — all residuals are non-dimensionalised to order unity, which takes the hyperparameter out of the problem rather than scheduling it.

Where the method loses — reported because it is what the experiment shows

⚠ The negative result

With 24 reference solutions over two parameters, a plain supervised model was twice as accurate in-envelope (6.70 K vs 13.59 K). The residual loss contains second derivatives of the network with respect to its inputs and is a substantially stiffer objective: it plateaus near 1.8 × 10⁻³ while the supervised runs reach about 2 × 10⁻⁵ at the shared budget. A dense design over two parameters also leaves little estimation error for a physics prior to remove.

I expect the balance to shift as parameter dimension grows, since design count for a fixed fill distance grows with dimension while Monte Carlo collocation does not — but that is an expectation, not a result of this work, and it is labelled as such in the manuscript.

Scope, stated plainly: one-dimensional conduction; coolant represented by a lumped film coefficient. No turbulence, no real-fluid properties, no heat transfer deterioration, no coking, no roughness, no channel-to-land lateral conduction. The surrogate is parametric, not an operator — it is conditioned on a fixed initial thermal state and cannot support receding-horizon re-planning. The contribution is methodological, demonstrated on a problem for which a verified reference is available.

02 / About

At the intersection of simulation and the flight line

Aerospace engineer with hands-on experience in UAV systems engineering, autonomous flight control and propulsion performance — currently reading for an MSc in Aeronautical Engineering at Linköping University, specialising in flight control systems and autonomous aerial vehicles.

My work sits where modelling meets the flight line. I design, model and validate aerial platforms in MATLAB/Simulink, ANSYS and CATIA V5, and prove them out through rigorous SIL/HIL pipelines before they ever leave the ground. I have built and flown fixed-wing UAVs, led multidisciplinary competition teams to three national podiums, and founded an aerospace start-up focused on propulsion-integrated UAV systems.

Alongside the hardware I do published research on model trustworthiness — specifically, on what has to be true before a fast surrogate model is allowed to sit inside a control or design loop. That work is sole-authored, fully open, and every quantitative claim in it is re-verified by an audit script against stored results.

I am open to entry- to mid-level roles in aeronautical engineering, UAV systems engineering, flight control or systems integration — based in Linköping and open to hybrid and on-site opportunities across Europe. No visa sponsorship required until August 2028.
